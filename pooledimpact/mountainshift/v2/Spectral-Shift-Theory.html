Spectral Shift Theory of Representation Flow

Version 6.0 · Will Fobbs · Pooled Impact · June 2026

Abstract

This paper develops a dynamical systems framework for understanding transformer inference as deterministic evolution in representation space.

Rather than analyzing learning dynamics through parameter updates, we analyze the evolution of internal representations during a forward pass. The central object of study is the shift field

$$F(S) = \phi(S) - S$$

where $S$ denotes the current representation and $\phi$ is the fixed inference operator induced by the model architecture and frozen parameters.

We show that:

  1. Inference defines a deterministic representation flow.
  2. Symmetry induces spectral compression.
  3. Spectral compression induces dimensional collapse.
  4. Effective rank evolves continuously under symmetry perturbation.
  5. A critical symmetry threshold exists.
  6. Repeated-token inputs define a degenerate geometric limit, termed the Lasagna Limit.
  7. The Lasagna Limit persists under perturbation as a slow manifold.
  8. Representation flow exhibits bifurcation behavior governed by the Jacobian spectrum of the shift field.

The resulting framework provides a geometric and spectral theory of inference independent of training dynamics.

1. Introduction

Transformer inference is commonly viewed as a sequence of matrix operations applied to token representations.

This perspective is computationally correct but obscures an important dynamical structure.

Each layer transforms a representation into a new representation:

$$S_{k+1} = \phi(S_k)$$

The representation therefore evolves through a sequence of deterministic state transitions.

The objective of this paper is to characterize the geometry of that evolution.

2. Representation Dynamics

Let

$$S_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$$

denote the initial representation.

Inference is defined recursively:

$$S_{k+1} = \phi(S_k)$$

where:

  • $n$ is sequence length,
  • $d$ is embedding dimension,
  • $\phi$ is fixed,
  • model parameters are frozen.

Inference is therefore a deterministic discrete dynamical system.

3. Shift Field

Definition 3.1

Define the shift field

$$F(S) = \phi(S) - S$$

The representation update becomes

$$S_{k+1} = S_k + F(S_k)$$

Definition 3.2

The layerwise shift is

$$\Delta S_k = S_{k+1} - S_k$$

Thus

$$\Delta S_k = F(S_k)$$

Theorem 3.1 (Existence of Shift)

For every inference step, $\Delta S_k$ exists.

Proof. Since both $S_k$ and $S_{k+1}$ are defined, $\Delta S_k = S_{k+1} - S_k$ is well-defined. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 3.2 (Deterministic Shift)

Given $S_0$, every shift is uniquely determined.

Proof. Since $\phi$ is fixed, $S_1 = \phi(S_0)$ is unique. By induction, $S_{k+1} = \phi(S_k)$ is unique for all $k$. Therefore $\Delta S_k$ is unique. $\blacksquare$

4. Jacobian of the Shift Field

Definition 4.1

The Jacobian of the shift field is

$$J_F(S) = \frac{\partial F(S)}{\partial S}$$

Since $F(S) = \phi(S) - S$, we obtain

$$J_F(S) = J_\phi(S) - I$$

where $I$ is the identity operator.

Interpretation

$J_F$ governs local deformation of representation space. Its spectrum determines:

  • contraction,
  • expansion,
  • stability,
  • bifurcation behavior.

5. Symmetry Parameter

Definition 5.1

Let $H(S)$ denote representation entropy. Define symmetry:

$$\sigma = 1 - \frac{H(S)}{H_{\max}}$$

where $0 \le \sigma \le 1$.

Interpretation

$\sigma \approx 1$ indicates strong redundancy. $\sigma \approx 0$ indicates high diversity.

6. Equivalence Class of Symmetry Measures

Different symmetry measures may be used. Let $\mathcal{F}$ denote the set of admissible symmetry functionals satisfying:

  • permutation invariance,
  • normalization,
  • stability under coarse graining.

Define $f_1 \sim f_2$ when $f_1 = a f_2 + b$ for constants $a, b$.

The symmetry space becomes

$$\Sigma = \mathcal{F} / \sim$$

Theorem 6.1

The theory depends only on equivalence classes in $\Sigma$.

Interpretation. No particular symmetry metric is privileged. The geometric structure is invariant under admissible transformations.

7. Effective Rank

Classical rank is discontinuous. We therefore define effective rank.

Definition 7.1

Let $p_i$ be normalized singular values of $J_F$. Define

$$r_{\text{eff}} = \exp\left(-\sum_i p_i \log p_i\right)$$

Interpretation

Effective rank measures spectral complexity. Unlike ordinary rank, it varies continuously.

8. Symmetry–Spectrum Coupling

Lemma 8.1

Increasing symmetry induces spectral concentration.

Statement. As $\sigma \uparrow$, the singular value spectrum concentrates. Consequently $r_{\text{eff}}$ decreases.

Proof sketch. At $\sigma = 1$ exactly, $S_0 = \mathbf{1}_n \otimes s$ and Theorem 9.1 forces $J_F(S_0)$ to rank one, so $r_{\text{eff}} \to 1$ at this boundary. For $\sigma = 1 - O(\delta)$, Theorem 9.2 gives $J_F(S_\delta) = J_F(S_0) + \delta D + O(\delta^2)$; the Davis–Kahan and Bauer–Fike bounds (Appendices A–B) control how the collapsed singular values separate from zero as $\delta$ grows, monotonically raising $r_{\text{eff}}$ as $\sigma$ falls from 1. $\blacksquare$

Corollary 8.1

The map $\sigma \mapsto r_{\text{eff}}$ is continuous except at the Phase II bifurcation boundaries of §14–15, where $\rho(J_F)$ crosses 1.

Corollary 8.2

Symmetry should be measured in representation space. Let $\sigma_{\text{token}}$ be token entropy and $\sigma_{\text{rep}}$ representation entropy. Then

$$\sigma_{\text{rep}} = \sigma_{\text{token}} + O(\delta)$$

where $\delta$ captures symmetry-breaking perturbations such as positional encoding and masking.

9. Permutation Symmetry and Near-Degenerate Manifolds

9.1 Exact Permutation Symmetry

Consider

$$S_0 = \mathbf{1}_n \otimes s$$

with identical token representations. Assume $\phi$ is permutation equivariant.

Theorem 9.1 (Exact Rank Collapse)

Under exact symmetry:

  • attention becomes rank one,
  • representation dynamics collapse onto an invariant manifold,
  • $J_F(S_0)$ collapses to rank exactly one — the case $r_{\text{eff}} \to 1$ anchoring Lemma 8.1: effective rank is not a side statistic here, it is the single number tracking how much of representation space is actually being used at any given symmetry level, from this rank-one floor up to full diversity in Phase III (§15).

9.2 Symmetry Breaking

Real transformers include:

  • positional encoding,
  • causal masking,
  • architectural asymmetries.

Represent the perturbed system as

$$S_\delta = S_0 + \delta P$$

where $P$ represents aggregate symmetry-breaking effects.

Theorem 9.2 (Near-Degenerate Spectral Persistence)

For sufficiently small $\delta$,

$$J_F(S_\delta) = J_F(S_0) + \delta D + O(\delta^2)$$

for some perturbation operator $D$. Consequently:

  1. Spectral splitting occurs continuously.
  2. Effective rank changes continuously.
  3. The degenerate manifold persists as a slow manifold.

Proof sketch. Apply: Davis–Kahan eigenspace perturbation bounds, Bauer–Fike eigenvalue perturbation bounds, Center Manifold persistence theory. $\blacksquare$

10. The Lasagna Limit

Definition 10.1

The Lasagna Limit is defined as $\delta \rightarrow 0$ in the perturbed system $S_\delta = S_0 + \delta P$.

Interpretation

The Lasagna Limit represents perfect redundancy. All semantic variation vanishes. Only the symmetry structure remains.

Theorem 10.1

The Lasagna Limit defines a degenerate invariant manifold.

Interpretation. Repeated-token inputs become a geometric limit object rather than a special case.

11. Lasagna Manifold Persistence

Definition 11.1

Let $M_0$ denote the degenerate manifold in the Lasagna Limit. For $\delta > 0$, define $M_\delta$ as the perturbed manifold.

Theorem 11.1

For sufficiently small $\delta$, $M_0$ persists as $M_\delta$ under perturbation.

Proof sketch. Center Manifold Theory. $\blacksquare$

12. Critical Symmetry

Definition 12.1

Let $S_\sigma$ denote the representation at symmetry level $\sigma$, i.e. $S_\sigma := S_{\delta(\sigma)}$ for whatever correspondence $\delta \leftrightarrow \sigma$ holds between the perturbation parameter of §9–11 and the symmetry parameter of §5 — a monotone bijection (justified informally by Lemma 8.1) is the simplest such correspondence but not asserted to be the only admissible one; see remarks following this review.

Define the spectral radius $\rho(J_F)$ and the critical symmetry

$$\sigma^* = \inf\{\sigma : \rho(J_F(S_\sigma)) \le 1\}$$

Interpretation

  • $\rho < 1$: contraction regime
  • $\rho = 1$: critical regime
  • $\rho > 1$: expansion regime

13. Spectral Gap Persistence

Theorem 13.1

Let $J_F(S_0)$ possess a dominant eigenspace separated by spectral gap $\gamma > 0$. Then for sufficiently small perturbation $\delta < \delta_c$, the spectral gap remains open, where $\delta_c$ denotes the same transition as $\sigma^*$ (§12) under whatever $\delta \leftrightarrow \sigma$ correspondence is in force — i.e. $\delta_c$ and $\sigma^*$ are one threshold, not two independent phenomena.

Interpretation

The dominant geometric structure survives symmetry breaking.

14. Spectral Bifurcation Theorem

Theorem 14.1

As symmetry varies, the Jacobian spectrum undergoes continuous deformation. When $\rho(J_F)$ crosses unity, a qualitative change in representation flow occurs.

Interpretation

Representation dynamics possess phase structure.

15. Representation Flow Phases

Phase I — Symmetry Dominated

  • high symmetry,
  • low effective rank,
  • strong contraction.

Phase II — Critical

  • spectral transition,
  • mixed stability,
  • bifurcation onset.

Phase III — Diversity Dominated

  • high effective rank,
  • expanded representation geometry,
  • increased dynamical freedom.

16. Main Theorem

Spectral Shift Theorem

Representation flow under a fixed operator $\phi$ is governed by symmetry-induced deformation of the shift-field spectrum. Specifically:

  1. Symmetry compresses spectrum.
  2. Spectral compression lowers effective rank.
  3. Effective rank governs accessible representation geometry.
  4. Symmetry breaking induces bifurcation.
  5. A critical symmetry threshold $\sigma^*$ exists.

Therefore representation dynamics exhibit phase structure determined by the Jacobian spectrum of the shift field. $\blacksquare$

17. Discussion

The central object of the theory is not the model weight matrix. The central object is

$$F(S) = \phi(S) - S$$

Inference is therefore understood as deterministic evolution through representation space.

The Lasagna Limit provides a symmetry anchor. The Jacobian provides local geometry. The spectrum provides phase structure. Together they define a spectral theory of representation flow.

Appendix A — Davis–Kahan Theorem

Used to bound eigenspace rotation under perturbation. Provides persistence guarantees for dominant symmetry subspaces.

Appendix B — Bauer–Fike Theorem

Used to bound eigenvalue movement under perturbation. Provides spectral splitting estimates for symmetry-breaking modes.

Appendix C — Center Manifold Theory

Used to establish persistence of the Lasagna manifold under small perturbations. Provides theoretical justification for slow manifold dynamics.

Conclusion

Inference is a deterministic dynamical system operating on representations rather than parameters.

The geometry of this system is governed by the shift field. The stability of the shift field is governed by its Jacobian. The Jacobian spectrum is governed by symmetry.

The resulting structure exhibits contraction, expansion, bifurcation, and manifold persistence.

This framework provides a mathematical foundation for analyzing representation dynamics independent of learning dynamics.
